A café counter in Pune. She scans the QR, enters her PIN and taps pay.

The spinner turns. Then it keeps turning.

Her bank buzzes: ₹1,250 debited. The café says nothing arrived. The app says pending.

Wait? Pay again? Call someone? Nothing on the screen says which one is safe.

An illustrated example. Amounts and the café are demo data.

The problem

Everyone knows something. No one explains.

Each one is telling the truth about what it can see.

None of them tells her what to do. I hover this diagram because that is the whole problem in one picture. A customer who is caught between a shop and an app shows up again and again in public complaints.

What goes wrong

Four small failures inside one stressful minute.

    What people say

    I went looking for other people in this moment. This is what turned up.

    Read three of these side by side and they say the same thing: wait, check, don’t retry. The app is the one place that could say it at the right moment.

    Honest notes: none of this is research I ran. No survey, no interviews, no usability test. Complaint sites skew angry, so these show the problem exists, not how often. Two of the complaints are from 2019 and the app has changed since.

    The app today

    What the existing screens say when it goes wrong.

    Five screenshots supplied for this case study, cropped so no transaction IDs show. They come from different dates and devices, so this is about how the app has spoken over time, not a verdict on today’s release.

    Across all five, the same three things keep coming back: try again later, a conditional note about the money, and a retry button. That is the gap this redesign is for.

    The opportunity

    I didn’t want to add features. I wanted one stretch of the product to finally make sense.

    Payment, then something goes wrong, then recovery. Four jobs for the screen.

      In scope: pending and failed states, status checking, outcomes, transaction details, refund information and support. Out of scope: the rest of the app, live bank integration, and promises the interface can’t verify.

      The insight

      She doesn’t need a bigger error message.

      A status is a label. What helps is what sits behind it. Pick one.

      Demo data. Wording follows the designed screens and has to be checked against real transaction states and current policy.

      The flow

      Check first. Then a receipt, or a way forward.

      Click any box to trace how someone ends up there. It plays on its own until you do.

      Understand

      Say what is happening before offering a button.

      Check

      Give a status check its own visible step.

      Confirm

      Show the outcome with its details.

      Recover

      Show the refund, the help and what comes next.

      The screens

      Eight screens, in the order she meets them.

      Reactions are design intent, not test results. The prototype’s outcome is simulated, with no live bank or PhonePe connection. Figma prototype: [add link before publishing]

      What this changes

      A calmer ending to a stressful moment.

      Instead of only saying the payment failed, the flow tells her what the status is, keeps her from paying twice, and shows what happens next.

      Clearer status. She can tell processing, attention, failed and paid apart.

      A safer next move. “Don’t pay again yet” sits right where the temptation starts.

      A real ending. A receipt on success, a refund tracker on failure.

      Help that already knows. Support starts from the payment she is looking at.

      These are expected benefits. Nothing here is measured.

      Validation, planned

      I haven’t tested this yet. Here is how I would.

      “You paid ₹1,250 to a merchant. The payment is still being processed. Show me what you would do next.”

      Watch for

      1. Can they explain what is happening?
      2. Do they know whether to pay again?
      3. Can they find the status check and help?
      4. Do they understand what happens if it finally fails?

      It works if they

      1. Understand the status on their own
      2. Choose the right next step
      3. Explain the refund timeline simply
      4. Know how the payment ended

      No participants recruited. No results exist.

      Small flow. Important moment.

      Next I’d check the wording against real bank states and current policy, test it with real people, and adapt it to the many ways a UPI payment can go wrong. Design for the moment people need answers most.
